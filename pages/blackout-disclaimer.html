What “Blackout” Really Means

How light behaves around a window treatment — and what to expect before you order

← Back to site

The short version. “Blackout” describes the fabric — it means the material itself does not pass light. It does not mean the finished window will be completely dark. Light still travels around the edges of almost every window treatment. If total darkness is your goal, read the section on side channel systems below, or just ask us — we will tell you honestly what a given product will and will not do in your room.

1. Understanding blackout

Every fabric we sell falls somewhere on a scale of how much light it lets through. The name describes the weave and coating of the material, measured on its own — not the performance of the installed window treatment.

Sheer
An open weave you can see through. Softens daylight and gives a hazy view out. Very little privacy after dark, when interior lighting reverses the effect.
Light Filtering
The most popular choice. Daytime privacy with a warm, diffused glow. Shapes and shadows may still be visible from outside at night.
Solar Screen
An engineered mesh rated by openness (1%, 3%, 5%, 10%). Cuts glare, heat and UV while preserving the view out. Deliberately not a privacy or darkening fabric.
Blackout
A coated or tightly woven fabric that blocks effectively all light through the material itself. Hold it up to a lamp and you will see nothing through it. Light can still enter around the edges once it is installed.
Full Blackout Side Channels
A blackout fabric combined with a channel system that captures the sides and a sealed cassette that closes the top. The closest thing to true darkness we offer.

The distinction that matters: blackout fabric is about the material. A blackout room is about the whole assembly — fabric, hardware, mount, and how it meets the window opening.

2. Blinds & shades

A shade is a flat panel that has to move, which means it must be slightly narrower than the space it travels in. That clearance is where light gets in.

Edge gaps are built in, not a defect

An inside mount shade is manufactured roughly a quarter to a half inch narrower than the window opening so it can raise and lower without binding. That leaves a light gap of about ⅛″ to ¼″ down each side. In a dark room facing a bright morning, those two thin lines are clearly visible. This is normal, it is how the product is engineered, and it is not grounds for a warranty claim.

An outside mount — where the shade is mounted on the wall above the window and overlaps the opening on all sides — reduces side light substantially. If darkness matters more to you than the built-in look, ask us about outside mount. It is usually the single most effective change you can make.

Other places light gets through

  • The top. An open roller tube leaves a gap between the fabric and the headrail. An enclosed cassette closes it.
  • The bottom. The hem bar rests against the sill rather than sealing to it.
  • Operating hardware. Cord routing, lift mechanisms and motor housings all need clearance.
  • Cellular shades. Blackout honeycomb fabric blocks light through the face, but the pleat structure and side seams can pass faint light. Top-down/bottom-up models add a second set of moving edges.
  • Horizontal blinds & shutters. These never darken a room. Even fully closed, light passes at every slat and around the tilt hardware. No slatted product is a blackout product, whatever the slat material.

3. Side channel systems

If you genuinely need a dark room — a nursery, a shift worker's bedroom, a media room, a migraine sufferer — the fabric alone will not get you there. A side channel system will get you close.

Channels are aluminum tracks mounted down both sides of the window. The edges of the shade ride inside them, so the side gaps are physically covered rather than merely narrow. Paired with a sealed cassette at the top, this is the only configuration we sell that approaches true darkness. On Norman roller shades we offer this as Full Blackout Side Channels.

What to expect from a side channel system. A dramatic improvement — most people describe it as the difference between “dim” and “dark.” You may still see a faint outline at the very top or bottom, and channels are visible hardware on the window frame, which changes the look. They also require adequate mounting depth and a blackout fabric to work; pairing channels with a light filtering fabric defeats the purpose.

4. Drapery & soft treatments

Blackout lining makes a real difference to a drapery panel, but fabric panels darken a room differently from a shade, and they have their own light paths.

  • Needle holes. Sewn panels are assembled with a needle. Every seam, hem and pleat leaves perforations, and against a bright window those can read as pinpoints of light. This is inherent to sewn goods.
  • Seams and hems. Wide windows need panels joined together; each seam is a potential light line.
  • The centre split. A pair of panels meets in the middle and light comes through where they overlap unless the pair is generously overlapped or held by a rod with an overlap master.
  • The top. Light passes over the rod unless the treatment is topped by a cornice, valance or ceiling-mounted track.
  • Returns and sides. Panels must wrap back to the wall at each end. Without a proper return, light spills around the outer edges.
  • Roman shades. Blackout lining is available, but the rings, rods and lift cords are stitched through the face fabric — those stitches show as small points of light.

A layered treatment — a blackout shade at the glass with drapery over it — consistently outperforms either one on its own, and it is what we recommend when a room needs to be genuinely dark and still look finished.

5. Your light conditions

Identical shades perform very differently in different rooms. Before deciding, consider:

  • Orientation. An east-facing bedroom takes direct sun at dawn — the hardest test there is. North-facing rooms are far more forgiving.
  • What is outside. Street lights, floodlights, headlights sweeping a wall, snow or a light-coloured patio reflecting sun upward.
  • The window itself. Deep sills, cranks, out-of-square openings and trim profiles all affect how closely a treatment can sit to the opening.
  • Light from inside. An open door, a hallway light or a bright screen will do more to light a room than a ¼″ edge gap.
  • Your eyes adapt. After twenty minutes in a dark room your pupils open and small amounts of light become far more noticeable than they were on first walking in.

6. No 100% guarantee

We do not guarantee complete darkness from any product we sell, including those described as blackout or fitted with full blackout side channels. We are telling you this before you order rather than after.

What we do stand behind: the fabric will be the light-blocking material it is sold as, the product will be built to the manufacturer's specification, and it will be free from defects in materials and workmanship under the warranty described in our Terms of Agreement.

What is not a defect: edge light gaps at the sides, top or bottom of a correctly manufactured treatment; light at seams, stitching or needle holes in sewn goods; light between slats or vanes; and light visible after your eyes have adjusted to a dark room. These are characteristics of window treatments, not faults, and they are not covered by warranty or grounds for return of a custom made-to-order product.
